Chapter 5

Asking questions

You can state facts all day. Getting a stranger on the phone to give you theirs takes a few more tools.

Ka-yan, calling about a roomAllô ? Bonjour. La chambre... à louer...

Mme Roy, the landlordOui, bonjour ! Vous avez une question ?

Ka-yan, thinkingI have nine questions. Price. Address. Metro. Cat. I know every word I need. I just don't know how to put them in a sentence that sounds like a question.

Ka-yanChambre... prix ? Métro... loin ? Chat... oui ?

Mme RoyDoucement, madame ! Une question à la fois.

Ka-yan, thinkingShe's laughing. Kindly, but still. I just ordered a room like a hot dog.

Doucement means "gently" or "slow down". Une question à la fois means "one question at a time". Good advice, and we'll take it. Ka-yan's problem isn't vocabulary. She has the nouns. What she's missing is the machinery: the little frames that turn a statement into a question.

The good news is that French gives you three frames for yes/no questions, and you can survive on the first one today. The even better news is that the question words (where, when, how much) are only about ten words, and you'll use them for the rest of your life.

Brain Power

Take the statement La chambre est libre. ("The room is free.") In English you can turn it into a question at least three ways: The room is free? / Is the room free? / Is it true that the room is free? Which one would you use with a friend? Which one would you write in an email to a landlord? Keep your answers. French has the same three, and the same split.

Yes or no: three ways, from casual to formal

Start with a sentence you can already make: Tu parles français. ("You speak French.") Here are the three ways to ask it.

WayHow it worksExampleUse it
1. IntonationSame words, voice goes up at the endTu parles français ?Speech with friends, quick questions
2. Est-ce quePut est-ce que in front, change nothing elseEst-ce que tu parles français ?
Est-ce que vous parlez français ?		Speech and writing. The safe default, including on the exam.
3. InversionSwap verb and pronoun, join them with a hyphenParles-tu français ?
Parlez-vous français ?		Formal writing, forms, polite or official speech

Way 1: raise your voice

No new words at all. You say the statement and let your voice climb on the last syllable. This is how most French people ask most yes/no questions in daily life. In writing, the question mark does the job.

Click it and listen to the rise: Vous habitez à Vancouver ? Now click the statement: Vous habitez à Vancouver. Same words. Only the tune changes.

Way 2: est-ce que

Est-ce que is literally "is it that". Est-ce que tu parles français ? is "Is it that you speak French?" It's a frame you stick in front of any statement. The word order inside doesn't move.

It's pronounced "es-keu". The t in est is silent (Chapter 1, Rule 1). Before a vowel, que shrinks to qu', because elision is compulsory:

Est-ce qu'que + il → qu'il ilvowel next, so elide habiteverb, unchanged ici ?"here"

So: Est-ce qu'il habite ici ? · Est-ce qu'elle parle français ? · Est-ce qu'on mange ? · but Est-ce que j'ai une chambre ? (j' counts as a consonant here, so que stays whole).

Way 3: inversion

Take the statement, flip the pronoun and the verb, and put a hyphen between them. Vous parlez becomes Parlez-vous. Three rules cover almost everything you'll meet:

  1. Hyphen. Always, between verb and pronoun: Habitez-vous ici ? · Avez-vous un chat ? · Êtes-vous libre ?
  2. The linking -t-. If the verb is written with a final -e or -a (parle, aime, a) and the pronoun is il, elle or on, French slips in a -t-. Go by the spelling, not the sound: parle sounds like it ends in l, but it's written with an e, so it's parle-t-il.
  3. Not with je. Nobody says parlé-je. With je, use est-ce que.
StatementInversion-t-?
Il parle français.Parle-t-il français ?yes, parle is written with a final e
Elle a un chat.A-t-elle un chat ?yes, a is written with a final a
Elle habite ici.Habite-t-elle ici ?yes
Il est libre.Est-il libre ?no, est already ends in a consonant
Ils ont un chat.Ont-ils un chat ?no, ont already ends in a consonant
Tu parles français.Parles-tu français ?no, tu isn't il/elle/on (the s stays)

The -t- is pronounced: Parle-t-il ? sounds like "parl-TIL". It's the sound that tells your ear an inversion is coming, so listen for it on the exam.

Negatives wrap around the whole pair, exactly like Chapter 4: N'avez-vous pas de chat ? ("Don't you have a cat?") and Ne parles-tu pas français ? The de after pas is the same de you learned there.

Inversion is swapping two arguments. Same function, different signature.

Which one, where?

Short version: when you speak, intonation and est-ce que are normal. Real inversion in everyday speech sounds a little stiff, like saying "Do you happen to possess a cat?" When you write formally or sit an exam, use est-ce que or inversion. Both are correct. Est-ce que is hard to get wrong, so pick it when in doubt.

中文腦：est-ce que 就係法文嘅「嗎」

普通話「你說法語嗎 (nǐ shuō fǎyǔ ma)？」同粵語「你識講法文呀 (nei5 sik1 gong2 faat3 man4 aa4)？」，都係喺句子加個語氣詞，字序完全唔變。Est-ce que 都係咁：加咗上去，後面照舊係 tu parles français。

分別只係位置：「嗎」「呀」喺句尾，est-ce que 喺句首。口語入面，淨係靠句尾語調上揚都問到，呢點同法文第一種（intonation）一樣。

Sharpen your pencil

Turn each statement into a question with est-ce que. Type the whole question. Watch the elision, and put a space before the question mark.

  1. Vous avez un chat.
  2. Elle habite à Montréal.
  3. Tu aimes le café.
  4. Ils cherchent une chambre.
  5. C'est loin.
  6. Il parle anglais.
Answers

Est-ce que vous avez un chat ? · Est-ce qu'elle habite à Montréal ? · Est-ce que tu aimes le café ? · Est-ce qu'ils cherchent une chambre ? · Est-ce que c'est loin ? · Est-ce qu'il parle anglais ?

Number 5 trap: que stays whole before c'est, because c' already counts as a consonant. Numbers 2, 4 and 6 need qu' because elle, ils and il start with a vowel sound.

The question words

These are the words that open a question when you need information, not a yes. You know the English set already: who, what, where, when, why, how. French has a set of the same size. Every qu- below is a plain "k" sound (the u is only a spelling placeholder), and quoi adds the [wa] from Chapter 1.

English wh- and French qu- are cousins: who/qui, when/quand, what/quoi share an ancient root.

WordMeansExample
quiwhoQui est-ce ? · Qui parle français ici ?
que, qu'est-ce quewhat (the thing the verb acts on)Qu'est-ce que tu cherches ? · Que cherchez-vous ?
quoiwhat (at the end, or after a preposition)Tu cherches quoi ?
oùwhereOù habitez-vous ?
quandwhenQuand est-ce que tu arrives ?
commenthowComment ça va ? · Comment vous appelez-vous ?
pourquoiwhy (the answer starts with parce que)Pourquoi est-ce que tu cherches une chambre ?
combien (de)how much / how manyÇa coûte combien ? · Combien de chambres avez-vous ?
quel, quelle, quels, quelleswhich / what + nounQuel est le prix ? (more below)

Combien de never takes an article after it: combien de chambres, not combien des chambres. It behaves like the de after pas in Chapter 4: quantity words are followed by bare de.

One question, three word orders

With a question word, you build the rest of the question the same way as before. Look at one question, Où habites-tu ?, in three outfits:

Oùquestion word est-ce queframe tusubject habites ?verb

OrderExampleUse it
question word + est-ce que + statementOù est-ce que tu habites ?
Où est-ce que vous habitez ?		Safe everywhere. Your default.

The third one is the easiest to build, and it's what you'll hear on the street. It's also a trap on the exam, where a formal speaker is expected. In the middle row, que becomes qu' before a vowel as usual: Quand est-ce qu'elle arrive ?

中文腦：疑問詞唔使搬位

中文嘅疑問詞企喺原位：粵語「你住喺邊度？」(nei5 zyu6 hai2 bin1 dou6)，普通話「你住在哪裡？」(nǐ zhù zài nǎlǐ)。口語法文一樣：Tu habites où ?，où 企喺「邊度」「哪裡」嗰個位。

所以三個語序入面，最似你母語嘅係第三個。日常傾偈夠用，但係考試或者打電話畀陌生人，用 Où est-ce que vous habitez ? 穩陣啲。

Watch it!

Qu'est-ce que c'est ? means "What is it?" Learn it as one block, said "kes-keu-sé". Literally it's "what is it that it is", but nobody parses it, and you will say it all the time.

Qu'est-ce que vs qu'est-ce qui. After que comes a new subject: Qu'est-ce que tu cherches ? (tu does the searching, the "what" is what you search). After qui the verb follows right away, because the "what" itself does the verb: Qu'est-ce qui est inclus ? ("What is included?") Quick test: a tu or vous next means que. A verb next means qui. That's all the qui-as-subject you need for now.

"What" before a noun is quel, not que. "What is the price?" is Quel est le prix ? Never Qu'est-ce que le prix. And plain que can't sit at the end of a question, so there it turns into quoi: Tu cherches quoi ?

Sharpen your pencil

Pick the question word that fits the reply.

  1. est-ce que tu habites ? → À Vancouver.
  2. ça va ? → Ça va bien.
  3. Tu arrives ? → Demain.
  4. Ça coûte ? → Dix dollars.
  5. est-ce que tu cherches un chat ? → Parce que j'aime les chats.
  6. est-ce ? → C'est Mme Roy.
  7. Tu cherches ? → Une chambre.
Answers

Où est-ce que tu habites ? · Comment ça va ? · Tu arrives quand ? · Ça coûte combien ? · Pourquoi est-ce que tu cherches un chat ? · Qui est-ce ? · Tu cherches quoi ?

Number 7 is the one that trips people: at the end of a question, "what" is quoi. At the start it would be Qu'est-ce que tu cherches ?

Quel: the one that agrees

Remember from Chapter 3 that adjectives agree with their noun? quel is an adjective ("which" or "what"), so it agrees too. It sits right before a noun, or before a form of être with the noun after it.

FormNoun is...Example
quelmasculine singularQuel est le prix ? · Quel âge avez-vous ?
quellefeminine singularQuelle est l'adresse ? · Quelle chambre est libre ?
quelsmasculine pluralQuels sont les jours libres ?
quellesfeminine pluralQuelles langues parlez-vous ?

All four sound identical: "kel". You can't hear the difference, so listening won't save you. You have to know the noun's gender and number, which is why Chapter 3 mattered. The only audible clue is liaison: the s of quels and quelles links to a following vowel, as in quels‿amis and quelles‿heures.

Sharpen your pencil

Choose the right form of quel. Check the noun first.

  1. est le prix ?
  2. est l'adresse ?
  3. langues parlez-vous ?
  4. sont les jours libres ?
  5. âge avez-vous ?
  6. chambre est libre ?
Answers

Quel est le prix (prix, masculine) · Quelle est l'adresse (adresse, feminine) · Quelles langues (langues, feminine plural) · Quels sont les jours (jours, masculine plural) · Quel âge (âge, masculine) · Quelle chambre (chambre, feminine)

Gender tip from Chapter 3: prix and âge are masculine even though nothing in the spelling says so. Learn nouns with their article.

Watch it!

Pourquoi is one word. Parce que is two. The question uses pourquoi, the answer uses parce que, and they never swap: Pourquoi tu cherches une chambre ? Parce que j'habite loin. Before a vowel, que elides: parce qu'il est loin.

Comment tu t'appelles ? is a block too. It means "What's your name?", but the literal words are "how do you call yourself". The t' is a reflexive pronoun, and reflexive verbs come later. For now: say Comment tu t'appelles ? with a friend, Comment vous appelez-vous ? to a stranger, and answer Je m'appelle Ka-yan.

There are no Dumb Questions

Do I really need inversion if est-ce que always works?
You need to recognise it. Forms, signs, emails and listening tasks are full of Avez-vous, Parlez-vous and Êtes-vous. For speaking, est-ce que is enough. If you use inversion, use it on easy verbs where you're sure of the hyphen and the -t-.
I've heard "Puis-je vous aider ?" Isn't that inversion with je?
Yes. A few fixed phrases keep it, and Puis-je vous aider ? ("May I help you?") is the famous one. Understand it, but don't build your own. With je, say Est-ce que je peux ... ?
What if the subject is a name, not a pronoun?
The name stays in front and a matching pronoun follows the verb: Mme Roy parle-t-elle anglais ? It's formal and mostly written. Est-ce que Mme Roy parle anglais ? says the same thing with less hassle.
Does my voice go up on every question?
On yes/no questions, yes, at the end. On questions with a question word, the voice usually stays level or falls at the end: Où est-ce que tu habites ? doesn't climb.

Answering: oui, non, and the third one, si

Oui means yes and non means no. Nothing new there. The surprise is a third word, si. You use it to say "yes" when someone asks a negative question and the real answer is yes.

Tu aimes le café ? → Oui, j'aime le café. / Non, je n'aime pas le café.

Tu n'aimes pas le café ? → Si, j'aime le café. / Non, je n'aime pas le café.

Notice that non still agrees with a negative statement. Only si turns it around. Saying oui to a negative question is the mistake to avoid.

You already know this

English doesn't have si. If someone asks "You don't like coffee?" and you say "Yes", it's unclear whether you like it or you agree you don't. German does have a word for it, doch. If you've met it ("Du magst keinen Kaffee?" "Doch!"), then si is the same idea. If you haven't, just remember: si = "yes, I do, you're wrong".

中文腦：你本身識答「si」嘅問題

人哋問你「你唔鍾意飲咖啡？」，粵語通常答「鍾意」或者「唔鍾意」(zung1 ji3 / m4 zung1 ji3)，即係重複個動詞。普通話都係：「喜歡」「不喜歡」 (xǐhuan / bù xǐhuan)。咁樣答就冇英文 "yes" 嗰種分唔清嘅問題。

法文用 si 解決：想答「鍾意」，就講 Si，然後重複動詞：Si, j'aime le café.

Sharpen your pencil

Read the question and the situation, then pick oui, non or si.

  1. Tu aimes le café ? (You love coffee.)
  2. Tu n'aimes pas le café ? (You love coffee.)
  3. Tu n'aimes pas le café ? (You hate coffee.)
  4. Vous avez un chat ? (You have no cat.)
  5. Vous n'avez pas de chat ? (You do have a cat.)
  6. Elle ne parle pas anglais ? (She doesn't speak English.)
Answers

1 oui (positive question, positive answer) · 2 si (negative question, but you do) · 3 non (negative question, and you don't) · 4 non · 5 si (Si, j'ai un chat.) · 6 non (Non, elle ne parle pas anglais.)

Rule: si only exists as a reply to a negative question, and only when the answer is positive.

Au Québec

In everyday Québec French you'll hear a fourth way to ask a yes/no question: a little -tu stuck onto the verb, like a spoken question mark.

  • Tu veux-tu un café ? ("Do you want a coffee?") The first tu is "you". The second is the particle.
  • C'est-tu loin ? ("Is it far?")

It works with any subject and it's purely spoken and informal. It's a bit like adding 嗎 or 呀 to the end of a sentence in Mandarin or Cantonese. Understand it when your landlord or coworker in Québec uses it, but don't use it on the exam, where est-ce que or inversion is what graders want.

Exam warm-up: phone the advertiser

In TEF Canada speaking, section A, you get a short ad and have to "phone" the advertiser (the examiner) to get more information. The examiner plays the person who placed the ad, so use vous, be polite, and start with Bonjour. Check the current timing and the number of questions expected on the official TEF Canada pages from the CCI Paris Île-de-France, because exam formats change.

Here's your ad. Click it to hear it.

Appartement à louer. Deux chambres. Métro à côté. Libre en mars. Téléphonez le soir.

(à louer = for rent · à côté = right next to it · libre = available · téléphonez = call us)

The ad tells you almost nothing, and that's the point. Write eight different questions you would ask, in full French, using vous. Try to use at least four different question words. No answer will be checked automatically, so say each one aloud and compare with the models.

Model questions
  1. Bonjour, madame. Est-ce que l'appartement est encore libre ? (Is it still available?)
  2. Quel est le loyer, s'il vous plaît ? (What's the rent?)
  3. Quelle est l'adresse ? (What's the address?)
  4. Qu'est-ce qui est inclus dans le loyer ? (What's included in the rent?)
  5. Est-ce que vous acceptez les chats ? (Do you accept cats?)
  6. Combien de temps dure le bail ? (How long is the lease?)
  7. Est-ce que je peux visiter l'appartement ? (Can I visit the apartment?)
  8. Comment vous appelez-vous ? (What's your name?)

Yours will differ, and that's fine. Any correct, polite question that gets you new information counts. Number 7 uses je peux ("I can"), from a verb we haven't covered yet. Learn Est-ce que je peux ... ? as a block for now.

Self-check: did you use vous every time? Did every est-ce que become est-ce qu' before a vowel? Did each quel match its noun? And did you ask questions you don't already know the answer to? The ad already told you the apartment has two bedrooms, so asking that earns nothing.

BULLET POINTS

  • Three ways to ask yes/no: rising voice (Tu parles français ?), est-ce que (Est-ce que tu parles français ?) and inversion (Parles-tu français ?). Speech uses the first two. The exam and formal writing want the second or third.
  • Est-ce que goes in front and changes nothing else. It becomes est-ce qu' before il, elle, on and ils.
  • Inversion: verb, hyphen, pronoun. Add -t- when a verb written with a final -e or -a meets il, elle or on (Parle-t-il ? A-t-elle ?). Not with je.
  • The question words are qui, que, quoi, où, quand, comment, pourquoi, combien (de), quel. The answer to pourquoi starts with parce que.
  • One question, three orders: Où est-ce que tu habites ? / Où habites-tu ? / Tu habites où ? The last stays in place, like Chinese question words, and belongs to casual speech.
  • Qu'est-ce que c'est ? and Comment tu t'appelles ? are blocks. Qu'est-ce que + subject, qu'est-ce qui + verb.
  • Quel, quelle, quels, quelles agree with the noun and all sound the same.
  • Reply si to say "yes" to a negative question. In Québec you'll hear -tu as a question marker: understand it, don't use it on the exam.
