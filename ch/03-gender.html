Chapter 3

Every noun has a gender

A table is a "she", a bed is a "he", and nobody can tell you why.

Mme Tremblay, landladyVoici le salon, voilà la cuisine, et là, une petite chambre calme. Il y a un balcon !

Ka-yan, nodding politelyLovely. Wonderful. Very nice.

Mme TremblayIl y a aussi une table et un lit. Le loyer est de 950 $ par mois.

Ka-yan, thinkingHang on. La table but le lit? Nothing about a table is female. And why "petite chambre" but "chambre calme"? Same sentence, two different rules.

Voici and voilà both mean "here is / there is". Il y a means "there is" or "there are". Learn it as one block.

Ka-yan has just met the thing that scares every beginner. Every French noun is masculine or feminine. A bed, a rent payment, a window, a whole building. Each noun carries a hidden label, and the label changes the little words around it.

This chapter gives you those little words (the articles), the adjectives that change to match, and the order they go in. By the end you can read an apartment ad and say what's wrong with the flat.

Reading ads, signs and leases is a big part of the reading tests, and tiny words like le and une are in every line.

Rule 1: every noun is masculine or feminine

For people, it follows the person. le père is a father, la mère a mother. For things, it's arbitrary. A window is feminine (la fenêtre), a balcony is masculine (le balcon), and no committee ever explained it.

Don't hunt for the logic. Learn each noun together with its article, as one word. Not "table", but la table. Write it that way in your notes and on your flashcards.

le litmasculine la tablefeminine le loyermasculine la cuisinefeminine

Click all four. ("The bed, the table, the rent, the kitchen".) Nothing about a bed is masculine. It just is.

Gender is a type system nobody asked for. Your job is to remember the type of every variable.

Rule 2: the little words (articles)

English has "the" and "a". French has the same two jobs, but each one comes in several shapes, and the shape tells you gender and number.

MasculineFemininePlural (both)

L' hides the gender

Remember elision from Chapter 1. Before a vowel or a silent h, le and la both squash into l'. So l'appartement (masculine) and l'adresse (feminine) look the same. The article stops telling you the gender, so learn those words with un or une instead: un appartement, une adresse. Never "le appartement". That's a spelling mistake.

You hear the plural in the little word, not the noun

Chapter 1 told you final -s is silent. So chambre and chambres sound identical. The plural lives in les and des, and in the liaison after them: an s that comes back as "z".

un appartement"un-na-par-te-man" les appartements"lé-za-par-te-man" des appartements"dé-za-par-te-man"

The "z" is the plural, spoken out loud. Now click both: La chambre est petite. / Les chambres sont petites. Only la / les and est / sont change. In the listening tests, train your ear for these tiny words. They carry the meaning.

Writing the plural is easy: add -s. Nouns in -eau take -x (un bureau → des bureaux) and nouns in -al become -aux (un journal → des journaux). The -s and -x are silent, but -al to -aux is one plural you can actually hear: "jour-nal" becomes "jour-no".

When do you use which? Roughly the way English does. un / une introduces something new, le / la points at something both of you already know.

Il y a une chambre. La chambre est petite.

Two things differ from English. French nearly always wants an article, so it's J'ai une table, never "J'ai table". And French uses le / la / les for things in general, where English uses nothing: J'aime les chats. ("I like cats.") Le loyer est cher à Vancouver. ("Rent is expensive in Vancouver.")

You already know this

English borrowed a huge pile of housing words from French, so many nouns come free: un appartement, une table, la cuisine (yes, "cuisine"), un balcon, le garage, la terrasse and le salon.

The meaning comes free. The gender doesn't, because English dropped it. Copy the article too.

Watch it!

Three false friends from the housing market.

  • une chambre is a bedroom, not a "chamber". (The House of Commons is la Chambre des communes, so the old meaning survives in Ottawa.)
  • le loyer is the rent. It looks like "lawyer" and has nothing to do with one. Your lawyer is un avocat or une avocate.
  • une location is a rental, not a place. A "place" is usually un endroit. (And la place means room, space, or a town square. Context will tell.)

Rule 3: some (du, de la, de l')

You can count a bed: one bed, two beds. You can't count coffee or space. For those uncountable things, French wants "some", called the partitive. It follows the same gender sorting.

There are no Dumb Questions

What happens if I get the gender wrong?
People understand you. Nobody has ever failed to rent a flat because they said le table. In speaking, graders mostly care that they can follow you. In writing, a wrong article or a wrong agreement counts as a grammar error, so it costs points if you do it over and over.
Is there any trick at all?
Three small ones. 1) Always learn nouns with their article. 2) Glance at the ending hints above. 3) Learn nouns inside a short phrase with an adjective you can hear change, like un petit lit / une petite table. Your ear stores that better than a label.
What about words for people?
They follow the person: un ami / une amie, un étudiant / une étudiante. Some don't change at all, just the article: un propriétaire / une propriétaire (landlord / landlady), un locataire / une locataire (tenant).
Does the gender ever change the meaning?
For a handful, yes. le poste is a position, la poste is the post office. le tour is a turn or a trip, la tour is a tower. Learn them as two different words.

Rule 4: adjectives agree with the noun

An adjective copies the gender and number of its noun. The dictionary form is masculine singular. For feminine, add -e. For plural, add -s. Listen to the pattern:

Le salon est grand.masculine: d silent La cuisine est grande.feminine: d wakes up Les fenêtres sont grandes.plural: you hear it in les, sont

That's the Chapter 1 trick again. grand has a silent d, and the -e wakes it up. If the masculine form ends in a silent consonant, the feminine is something you can hear. petit / petite, vert / verte, chaud / chaude.

Agreement is the type checker. It runs on every sentence, and it never gets tired.

Rule 5: most adjectives come after the noun

English puts the adjective first: a quiet apartment. French usually puts it second: un appartement calme. Colours, nationalities and most descriptions follow the noun.

A short list of common adjectives goes before the noun. Remember them as BAGS: Beauty, Age, Goodness, Size.

Watch it!

C'est + adjective is always masculine. When you comment on a whole situation, with no noun in the sentence, the adjective stays masculine singular: Le loyer ? C'est cher ! · C'est petit, mais c'est calme.

The moment you name the thing, the adjective agrees with it: La chambre est chère. / Elle est chère. And after c'est plus a noun, the adjective agrees with that noun: C'est une belle cuisine.

Au Québec

Housing is where you'll meet Québec French first.

  • Apartments are counted in rooms. un 3 1/2 is said "un trois et demi". The 3 are the kitchen, the living room and the bedroom. The bathroom is the 1/2. A 4 1/2 usually has a second bedroom. It's masculine because an unspoken appartement or logement sits behind it.
  • un logement (a home you rent) is standard French, but in Québec ads it's everywhere: logement à louer.
  • le dépanneur, "the dép", is the corner store. It's open late and it sells milk, chips and lottery tickets.
  • la job. Québec speech often makes this word feminine: J'ai une bonne job. In France the same borrowing is masculine. On the exam, use le travail or un emploi.

Understand them all. On the exam, stick to the standard words.

BULLET POINTS

  • Every noun is masculine or feminine, and for things it's arbitrary. Learn each noun with its article: la table, le lit.
  • The article is a bit like a Chinese measure word: it goes with the noun, and you learn the pair.
  • Definite: le / la / l' / les. Indefinite: un / une / des. l' hides the gender, so learn those with un or une.
  • Plural -s is silent. You hear plural in les, des and the liaison "z".
  • For "some" of something uncountable: du café, de la place, de l'eau.
  • Endings are hints, not laws: -tion, -té, -ure, -ette, -ence are usually feminine. -ment, -age, -eau, -isme are usually masculine. Remember la plage, la page, l'eau, la peau.
  • Adjectives agree. Add -e for feminine and -s for plural. The silent consonant wakes up in the feminine. Adjectives already ending in -e don't change.
  • Watch the patterns: -eux/-euse, -if/-ive, -ien/-ienne, -er/-ère, and the irregulars beau / bel / belle, nouveau / nouvel / nouvelle, vieux / vieil / vieille.
  • Most adjectives go after the noun. BAGS (beauty, age, goodness, size) go before.
  • C'est cher !: after c'est, a bare adjective stays masculine.
  • False friends: chambre is a bedroom, loyer is rent, location is a rental.
