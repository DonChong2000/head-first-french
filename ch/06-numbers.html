Numbers, time, dates, prices

Every voicemail in Canada is a number said at double speed. Time to slow it down.

Ka-yan, Montréal, a voicemail from the pharmacyShe presses play. The message lasts nine seconds.

Ka-yan, thinkingSoixante-treize: sixty and thirteen, fine. Quatre-vingt-quinze: four... twenties? And fifteen? I'm still doing sums and the machine has already moved on. Closing at dix-neuf heures. Nineteen o'clock? Is that a thing?

Ordonnance means prescription and prête means ready. Everything else in that message was a number. That's normal. Prices, phone numbers, opening hours and appointment dates are what real French is made of when you're new in Canada, and they're also what TEF and TCF listening tasks love to test.

Chapter 2 gave you 0 to 20. This chapter builds the rest, then uses it for money, phone numbers, the clock and the calendar. The intro promised you'd be annoyed by something in this book. This is the page.

Numbers are where listening exams are won or lost, because you can't guess a digit from context.

21 to 69: the easy bit

Good news first. From 20 to 69, French works like English: a tens word, then the unit.

TensFrenchSound note
20vingtNasal, no t, when it stands alone.
30trenteNasal vowel, then a real t. The -e wakes it up, as in Chapter 1.
40quaranteSame ending as trente.
50cinquanteSame ending again.
60soixanteThe x sounds like s: "swa-SANT".

Add a hyphen and a unit: vingt-deux (22), trente-cinq (35), quarante-huit (48), cinquante-neuf (59), soixante-six (66). Click them all.

One exception. When the unit is one, you say et un instead of using a hyphen: vingt et un (21), trente et un (31), quarante et un (41), cinquante et un (51), soixante et un (61). With a feminine noun, it's une: trente et une minutes.

vingtt silent vingt-deuxt said: "vin-t-deux" vingt et unt said before et

That's the one sound trap. vingt keeps its t silent when it stands alone or before a consonant (vingt dollars), but you hear it inside 21 to 29 and before a vowel (vingt ans).

You already know this

quarante is the root of "quarantine", which began as forty days of waiting. cent is in "percent" and "century". million is the same word in both languages. dix and "decimal" share the same Latin ancestor.

French doesn't have words for 70, 80 and 90. It builds them from 60 and 20. Yes, really.

Watch it!

You'll hear septante and nonante. In Belgium, and in parts of French-speaking Switzerland, 70 is septante and 90 is nonante. Some Swiss areas also say huitante for 80. They're simple (septante-cinq is 70 + 5), and they're what Old French used too. Québec uses the France forms from this chapter, and so do TEF and TCF. Recognise septante and nonante if you hear them from a Belgian or Swiss speaker, but write and say the standard ones.

There are no Dumb Questions

Why on earth does French count like this?
Nobody is completely sure. The usual story is a leftover of counting in twenties, which some old European languages did, and historians still argue about where it came from. Medieval French had septante and nonante too. In France the twenty-based forms won, and the others survived only in Belgium and Switzerland. A Paris hospital, the Quinze-Vingts, is named after 15 × 20 = 300 beds.
Why vingt et un but quatre-vingt-un?
Because that's the tradition. The et shows up for 21, 31, 41, 51, 61 and 71, and then stops. After quatre-vingt it's always a hyphen. Learn it as six et numbers and you're done.
Is it OK to say septante on the exam?
It would be understood, but why take the risk? The rest of the exam, and the people you'll meet in Canada, use soixante-dix.

Prices: what the dépanneur says

Here's the cash register at a corner store. Three ways to ask, one way to answer.

  • C'est combien ? or Ça coûte combien ? ("How much is it?") or Ça fait combien ? ("What does that come to?")
  • Ça fait quatre dollars soixante-quinze. ("That's $4.75.")
  • Le café coûte deux dollars vingt-cinq.

Now the format. Canada writes the amount first, then a space, then the sign.

WrittenSaidNote

Price tags often say what the tax is doing: taxes incluses (tax included), avant taxes (before tax) or plus les taxes (tax on top). In France the same pattern works with euros: 12,50 € is douze euros cinquante.

Say the cents in full. 12,50 $ is douze dollars cinquante, not douze dollars cinq. That would be $12.05. And on signs in Québec you'll see 12,50 $, 12.50$ and $12.50. They all mean the same thing, and you read them all the same way.

Au Québec

Two money words you'll hear at the dépanneur and the market:

  • Piasse is an informal dollar, from the old word piastre. Ça coûte dix piasses. It's chatty and friendly, a bit like saying "bucks".

Time: what the clock says

Two common ways to ask: Quelle heure est-il ? (the textbook one) and Il est quelle heure ? (what people say). To be polite to a stranger: Vous avez l'heure, s'il vous plaît ? The answer starts with Il est.

The clock has two systems. In everyday talk, French goes round a 12-hour clock with et quart, et demie and moins. On schedules, appointment cards, bus timetables and in exam recordings, you get the 24-hour clock, written in Canada as 15 h 30 and said with plain numbers.

WrittenEveryday24-hour, said

3 h 30trois heures et demiequinze heures trente

Four things hold the system up.

  • Past the half hour you count backwards. 3 h 40 is "four o'clock minus twenty": quatre heures moins vingt. The hour goes up by one, then you subtract.
  • heure is singular only for one. Il est une heure. but Il est deux heures. After une heure you can still say et quart.
  • demie with e, demi without. heure is feminine, so trois heures et demie. midi and minuit are masculine, so midi et demi. Same sound either way.
  • "At" is à. Le cours commence à huit heures. Le bus part à midi. Le magasin ferme à dix-huit heures. To ask when, put À quelle heure first: À quelle heure est le rendez-vous ? Or leave it for last: Le bus part à quelle heure ?

(The table uses afternoon times, so 3 h is quinze heures.) For the 24-hour clock, you subtract 12 from anything after noon: 18 h is 6 pm, dix-huit heures. That was the pharmacy closing at dix-neuf heures trente, which is 7:30 pm. Ka-yan had 40 minutes after all. (If you need to say "in the morning", it's du matin. Afternoon is de l'après-midi, evening is du soir.)

Watch it!

Don't mix the two systems. You say trois heures et quart or quinze heures quinze. Not quinze heures et quart. And with the 24-hour clock there is no moins: 15 h 45 is quinze heures quarante-cinq.

The number sounds change here. six‿heures has a z, dix‿heures has a z, neuf‿heures becomes "neuv-eur", and huit‿heures keeps the t. This is the pronunciation table from earlier doing real work.

Dates: day, month, year

French dates go in one order: day, month, year. Days of the week and months are written with a small letter, always.

Days: lundi, mardi, mercredi, jeudi, vendredi, samedi, dimanche.

Months: janvier, février, mars, avril, mai, juin, juillet, août, septembre, octobre, novembre, décembre.

The pattern is le + number + month. Use plain numbers, with one exception: the first of the month is an ordinal.

le premier1 only maismall letter    le deuxplain number maismall letter    le quinzeplain number août"oot"

Click them: le premier mai, le deux mai, le quinze août. Add the year at the end: le quinze août deux mille vingt-six. To ask for today's date, say Quelle est la date aujourd'hui ? The answer is Nous sommes le dix-sept octobre. (People also say On est le dix-sept octobre.)

Written short, you'll see le 1er mai and le 2 mai. The little er is the sign that it's premier.

Watch it!

Never elide before huit and onze. It's le huit mars and le onze novembre, not l'onze. huit starts with an h aspiré (Chapter 1), and onze is a special case that behaves the same way. Same goes for no liaison before them: les onze has no z.

lundi is not le lundi. Le rendez-vous est lundi. means this coming Monday. Le magasin est fermé le lundi. means every Monday. That one little le is the difference between once and always.

Careful with numbers on forms

Canadian forms use several numeric formats. You'll see JJ/MM/AAAA (jour, mois, année), AAAA-MM-JJ (year first, like 2026-10-17), and some English-style forms with the month first. So 03/04/2026 can be 3 April or March 4. If the form lets you write words, write the month in full: le 3 avril 2026. If it's a box format, check the labels before you fill it in.

Ordinals: first, second, third

Ordinals come from the number plus -ième: deuxième (2nd), troisième (3rd), quatrième (4th, the e of quatre drops), cinquième (5th, a u appears), neuvième (9th, f becomes v), dixième (10th). The first one is different: premier (masculine) and première (feminine). second and seconde also exist, but deuxième is always safe.

C'est ma première semaine. Elle habite au troisième étage. premier and première agree in gender. The -ième ones stay the same. A warning for floors: counting étages differs between France and Canada, since in France the rez-de-chaussée is the ground floor and le premier étage sits above it. When it matters, ask which floor, or look at the elevator.

Au Québec

Three calendar dates you'll hear in Montréal:

  • le vingt-quatre juin is the Fête nationale of Québec.
  • le premier juillet is the fête du Canada. It's also the traditional moving day for rented apartments, because many leases used to end on June 30.
  • le onze novembre is Remembrance Day. In French it's le jour du Souvenir.

There are no Dumb Questions

Will I really hear 24-hour time in the exam?
Often, yes. Schedules, appointment reminders and announcements tend to use it, because it avoids am and pm. Real life in Canada does the same: bus schedules and appointment cards say 14 h 30. Say it with plain numbers (quatorze heures trente) and you're safe.
Do I say numbers differently in Canada and in France?
Almost never. The big differences are the price format, the words piasse and sou, and how phone numbers are read. The counting itself is the same, and so is the 70 to 99 system.
What if I forget the number halfway through a sentence?
Say it in smaller pieces. Soixante... et onze. is better than silence. In the speaking exam, a short pause and a correct number beats a fast wrong one.

  • 70 to 79 are soixante + 10 to 19. 80 to 99 are quatre-vingt + 0 to 19. Only quatre-vingts (80) has the s.
  • Belgium and Switzerland say septante and nonante. Québec and the exams use the France forms.
  • cent and vingt take an s only when multiplied and last: deux cents, deux cent un. mille never takes an s. million is a noun with de.
  • six and dix end in "s" alone, silent before a consonant, "z" before a vowel. neuf becomes "neuv" before ans and heures.
  • Prices: 12,50 $ is douze dollars cinquante. Comma for decimals, space for thousands, $ at the end. Ask C'est combien ?. Québec: piasse and sou.
  • Phone numbers: pairs in France, mostly single digits in Canada, and both are used.
  • Time: et quart, et demie, moins le quart, moins dix, midi, minuit. The 24-hour clock (15 h 30) uses plain numbers, no moins.
