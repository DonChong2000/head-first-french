Chapter 4

-ER verbs and saying no

Five spellings, three sounds, and a tiny word that vanishes the moment people start talking.

Simon, a coworkerSalut ! Tu es la nouvelle ? Moi, c'est Simon. Tu habites à Montréal ?

Ka-yan, first day at workOui, j'habite à Verdun.

SimonMoi, j'adore le café. Mais ici, y a pas de café !

Ka-yan, thinking"Tu habites", "j'habite": the verb sounds exactly the same. So why does the textbook spell it two ways? And where did the ne go? I learned ne ... pas. He only said pas.

Good news first. Ka-yan's questions are really one question: what do I actually have to say, and what do I only have to write? This chapter answers it for the biggest family of verbs in French, the ones that end in -er. Then it teaches you to say no, which is a useful skill at work.

About nine French verbs in ten end in -er. Learn one pattern, get thousands of verbs.

One pattern for thousands of verbs

Take the infinitive (the dictionary form), chop off -er, and add an ending. That's the whole machine. Start with parler (to speak). The stem is parl-.

Five spellings, three sounds

Four of the six forms sound identical. Chapter 1 already told you why: a final -e, -es and -ent on a verb are all silent. Here they are with the silent letters marked.

Je parle-e silent tu parles-es silent il parle-e silent ils parlent-ent silent

All four are just "parl". Now the two that do show up in your ears:

nous parlonsparl-ON, nasal vous parlezparl-AY

So in speech an -er verb has three forms: "parl", "parl-ON", "parl-AY". In writing it has five spellings. When you listen, the pronoun (tu, il, ils ...) tells you who it is, not the ending. When you write, you have to supply the right ending yourself.

Written endings are type annotations. The compiler (whoever reads your writing) checks them. The runtime (speech) mostly ignores them.

Elision and liaison on the job

Chapter 1 showed you how short words glue to the next word. Verbs that start with a vowel or a silent h do both tricks all day long.

Elision: je becomes j'

J'habiteje + habite j'aimeje + aime j'écouteje + écoute j'arriveje + arrive j'étudieje + étudie

Only je elides here. tu aimes, il aime and elle habite keep their full pronouns. Writing "je habite" is a spelling mistake, and graders notice.

Liaison: the z that tells ils from il

nous habitonsnou-za-bi-ton vous écoutezvou-zé-cou-té ils aimentil-zèm elles arriventel-za-riv

Pronoun plus verb always links. For nous, vous, ils and elles that gives you a [z] (the pronoun's silent s waking up). It is also your best clue for hearing plural: il aime vs ils aiment.

Watch it!

tu and je never link. tu has no silent consonant to wake up, and je has already lost its vowel. And the z only appears when a vowel sound follows: ils parlent has no z, so it sounds exactly like il parle.

When the stem changes spelling

A few -er verbs tweak their spelling so the sound stays the same. There are six you need now, and the pattern is easy to spot.

VerbThe changeWhereExample
mangerg becomes genousnous mangeons (but ils mangent)
commencerc becomes çnousnous commençons (but ils commencent)
payery becomes i (y is also accepted)je, tu, il, ilsje paie or je paye, ils paient, but nous payons
achetere becomes èje, tu, il, ilsj'achète, ils achètent, but nous achetons
appelerl becomes llje, tu, il, ilsj'appelle, ils appellent, but nous appelons
préféreré becomes èje, tu, il, ilsje préfère, ils préfèrent, but nous préférons

manger and commencer only change before -ons, to keep the g and the c soft. Without the extra letter, "nous mangons" would say a hard g, and "nous commencons" a hard k. Remember the cedilla from Chapter 1: ç makes c sound like s before a, o or u.

The other four change in exactly the four forms whose endings are silent (je, tu, il, ils), the same four that sound alike. With a silent ending, the stem is the last thing you hear, so the weak e turns into a full è: j'achète is "ja-chèt", but nous achetons is "nou-za-ch'-ton". You don't need the theory. Just remember that nous and vous keep the plain stem.

Liking things: aimer, adorer, détester, préférer

Opinions at work come up all the time: the coffee, the meetings, the snow. These four verbs take the definite article (le, la, l', les) when you mean something in general.

  • J'aime le café.
  • Elle adore la neige.
  • Je déteste les réunions.
  • Nous préférons l'hiver.

You can also put a second verb straight after them. The second verb stays in the infinitive. Only the first one changes.

  • J'aime travailler ici.
  • Elle adore manger avec les collègues.
  • Je déteste arriver en retard.
  • Tu préfères rester à la maison ?

Watch it!

Likes take le, not du. In Chapter 3, du and de la meant "some". That's for eating, buying and having: Je mange du pain. (I'm eating some bread.) But a like is about the whole idea of something, so it takes the definite article: J'aime le pain. (I like bread.) J'aime du pain sounds wrong to a French ear.

There are no Dumb Questions

If tu parles and il parle sound the same, why does French bother with different spellings?
Because they weren't always the same. In medieval French many of those endings were still pronounced. Speech moved on, and the spelling stayed behind. For you, that means the ear can't help you with endings, so learn the pattern once and write by pattern. Graders mark ending mistakes as grammar errors, even though a listener would never notice them.
What about vous parlez, parler and parlé?
All three sound like "parlé". That's another reason spelling matters. You'll learn how to tell them apart when we get to the past tense, so for now just know it's coming.
Is J'aime Marc OK for a coworker?
Careful. With a person, plain aimer often means love. For a coworker you like, say J'aime bien Marc. With things it's fine: J'aime le café is just a normal liking.
Can I text je aime?
No. J'aime, always. Elision is compulsory, even in texts.

Saying no: ne ... pas

French wraps the verb in two words. ne goes before the verb, pas goes right after it.

Jewho neno, part 1 parlethe verb, in the middle pasno, part 2 cantonais.the rest

Click it: Je ne parle pas cantonais. Before a vowel or a silent h, ne loses its e, exactly like je does:

  • Je n'aime pas le café.
  • Il n'habite pas à Ottawa.
  • Nous n'écoutons pas la radio.

With two verbs in a row, only the first one gets wrapped: Je n'aime pas travailler le dimanche. The infinitive stays outside, unwrapped.

After a no, un and des become de

Here's a rule that surprises everyone once. When you say you don't have something, the article in front of it (un, une, des, du, de la) shrinks to de, or d' before a vowel.

PositiveNegativeWhat changed
J'ai une voiture.Je n'ai pas de voiture.une → de
Elle a un emploi.Elle n'a pas d'emploi.un → d'
Nous avons des questions.Nous n'avons pas de questions.des → de
Tu manges du pain.Tu ne manges pas de pain.du → de
Il mange de la viande.Il ne mange jamais de viande.de la → de (same for jamais and plus)

The idea: the article counts how many you've got, and after a no, the answer is zero. So nothing is left to count, and the plain de ("of") is all that remains.

粵語嘅「唔」m4 就係最簡單嘅 ne ... pas：我唔鍾意 ngo5 m4 zung1ji3 ≈ Je n'aime pas. 「冇」mou5 即係「唔有」，正正就係 avoir 加 ne ... pas：我冇車 ngo5 mou5 ce1 ≈ Je n'ai pas de voiture. 一諗到「冇」，就記住法文個冠詞要縮做 de。

普通話：我不喜歡 wǒ bù xǐhuan、我沒有車 wǒ méi yǒu chē，道理一樣。小心：「冇」只係「有」嘅反面。其他動詞要用「唔」，例如我唔食肉 ngo5 m4 sik6 juk6，但法文照樣要 de：Je ne mange pas de viande.

Watch it!

Two cases where the article does not become de.

  • être (to be). C'est un problème. → Ce n'est pas un problème. You are describing what something is, not counting a quantity, so un stays.
  • The likes from the section before. J'aime le café. → Je n'aime pas le café. A definite article (le, la, l', les) never turns into de. Only un, une, des, du, de la do.

What people actually say: the ne that vanishes

Back to Simon. In real conversation, ne almost always disappears and pas does all the work.

Write this (exam)Hear this (everyday)
Je n'aime pas ça.J'aime pas ça.
Je ne sais pas. (I don't know)Je sais pas.
Il n'y a pas de café. (There isn't any coffee)Y a pas de café.
Je ne travaille pas demain.Je travaille pas demain.

That's what Simon did: y a pas de café is the casual version of il n'y a pas de café. Notice that de survived. Dropping ne doesn't change anything else.

Listening tip: don't wait for ne. Listen for pas, jamais, plus or rien after the verb. Writing tip: always put ne in. Written French, and that includes the writing tasks, expects the full pair.

Au Québec

Dropping ne is the default in Québec, in the office and on the radio. A few more things you'll hear on your first week:

  • pantoute means "not at all" (pas du tout). J'aime pas ça pantoute.
  • ben is bien, said fast. J'aime ben ça, le café. (I like coffee a lot.)
  • The verb is the same, travailler, but the noun for work is la job, and it's feminine: Je cherche une job. On the exam, write un emploi or du travail instead.

BULLET POINTS

  • Regular -er verbs: drop -er, add -e, -es, -e, -ons, -ez, -ent.
  • Five spellings, three sounds. je / tu / il / ils parle(nt) all sound alike, only nous parlons and vous parlez differ. Write the endings, listen for the pronouns.
  • Elision: j'habite, j'aime, j'écoute. Liaison after a plural pronoun gives a z: ils aiment vs il aime.
  • Spelling changes: mangeons, commençons, paie, achète, appelle, préfère. nous and vous keep the plain stem, except that manger and commencer change at nous only.
  • Likes take the definite article: J'aime le café. After a like, a second verb stays in the infinitive: J'aime travailler.
  • ne goes before the verb and pas after it. n' before a vowel. Also jamais, plus, rien.
  • After a negation, un, une, des, du, de la become de / d': Je n'ai pas de voiture. Exceptions: être and the definite article.
  • In speech ne disappears (J'aime pas ça). Understand it, but write ne on the exam.
