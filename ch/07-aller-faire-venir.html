Chapter 7

Aller, faire, venir: near future, near past

Three small verbs that say where you're going, what you're doing, and where you just came from.

Simon, Friday, 4:45 p.m.Salut, Ka-yan ! Qu'est-ce que tu fais ce week-end ?

Ka-yan, thinking"Qu'est-ce que" is Chapter 5, fine. But "tu fais"? And he used the present tense for a weekend that hasn't happened yet. I'll answer with what I know.

Ka-yanEuh... Je suis au cinéma. Samedi.

SimonAh, tu vas au cinéma ! Super. Moi, je vais à la bibliothèque, et après, je vais faire les courses. Tu viens ?

Ka-yanOui ! Je viens de Hong Kong !

SimonEuh... non. Tu viens avec moi ?

Ka-yan, thinkingI told him I'm sitting in a cinema. Right now, in the office. Then I answered "Are you coming?" with my hometown. The only thing I recognised was "ça va", hiding inside "tu vas".

Ka-yan made two classic mistakes. Je suis au cinéma means "I am at the cinema" (a place, right now). What she wanted was "I'm going to the cinema". And Je viens de Hong Kong is a perfectly good sentence, just not an answer to Tu viens ? ("Are you coming?").

This chapter fixes both. You'll learn three verbs that make up half of any weekend conversation: aller (to go), faire (to do, to make) and venir (to come). Then comes the best bonus in French grammar. Put aller in front of another verb and you get the future. Put venir de in front and you get the recent past. No new endings, no new tenses to memorise.

Spoken French uses "aller + verb" for the future far more than the real future tense. Learn this one first.

Brain Power

English asks "How's it going?" and answers "It's going well". French asks Ça va ? and answers Ça va bien. Two languages, one verb, no coordination. Why do you think both of them picked "go" to ask about your health? Think of one more English phrase where "go" talks about something that isn't a journey.

Watch it!

au, à la, à l', aux: the little merge

When you go somewhere, you need à ("to", "at") plus the place. If the place has a le or les, the two words merge. It isn't optional, just like the elision from Chapter 1.

à + le = au · à + les = aux · and à la, à l' stay as they are. The word de ("of", "from") does the same thing, and you'll need it for venir in a minute.

Cities, countries and how you get there

Places on a map follow a short rule. English says "in" or "to" for everything. French splits it four ways.

Watch it!

There are no Dumb Questions

Why en voiture but à vélo? Is there a rule?
A rule of thumb: if you sit inside, it's en. If you ride on top of it or use your body, it's à. It breaks sometimes, and you'll hear en vélo in casual speech. On the exam, stay with à vélo.
How do I say "I'm going to Simon's place"?
Use chez, one word that means "at (someone's) place": Je vais chez Simon. · Tu viens chez moi ? · Elle va chez le dentiste. No article merge, no à.
After je vais, can I put a second verb?
Yes, and that's the future. We'll get to it as soon as you've met faire and venir.

This is the verb behind Simon's question, and it's one of the most useful verbs in French. It means "to do" and "to make", and it carries about a hundred fixed expressions.

The weather: Il fait...

French weather talk also uses faire. The subject is always il, and it means nothing, just like English "it" in "it's cold".

  • Il fait froid. (It's cold.)
  • Il fait chaud. (It's hot.)
  • Il fait beau. (The weather is nice.)
  • Il fait mauvais. (The weather is bad.)

A few weather words have their own verbs: Il neige. (It's snowing.) Il pleut. (It's raining.) Canadians need both, usually in the same week.

Watch it!

J'ai froid and Il fait froid are not the same sentence. Chapter 2 gave you J'ai froid: I feel cold. Il fait froid says the weather is cold. A person can't "make" cold, and the weather can't "have" cold. Mixing them up gives you Je fais froid (a very strange claim about yourself) or Il a froid (he's cold, which is fine, but it's not the weather).

Futur proche: aller + infinitive

Here is the bonus. Take aller, conjugate it, and put any infinitive after it. That's the "near future", the futur proche. It means "I'm going to ...".

Jewho vaisaller, conjugated travaillerinfinitive, never changes demain.when

Click it: Je vais travailler demain. More: Tu vas manger au restaurant. · Elle va arriver ce soir. · Nous allons faire les courses. · Ils vont visiter Québec. · Il va faire beau.

Futur proche is a wrapper function. aller handles the conjugation and the person. The infinitive does the real work and never changes.

Only the first verb changes. The second stays in the dictionary form, which is the same trick as J'aime travailler from Chapter 4. Writing je vais travaille is the classic mistake.

Saying no, asking questions

ne ... pas wraps aller, the conjugated verb, and leaves the infinitive outside, exactly like Chapter 4: Je ne vais pas travailler. · Elle ne va pas venir. · Nous n'allons pas manger ici.

Questions from Chapter 5 work too: Tu vas travailler samedi ? · Est-ce que vous allez venir ? · and Simon's question in the future version, Qu'est-ce que tu vas faire ce week-end ?

You already know this

English does exactly the same thing: "I'm going to work tomorrow". "Go" turns into a helper that points at the future. One bonus: English can't tell "I'm going to work" (the place) from "I'm going to work" (the activity). French can. Je vais au travail is the place. Je vais travailler is the activity.

The mirror trick. Conjugate venir, add de (or d' before a vowel), then an infinitive. It means "I just ...".

Watch it!

"Just" is a short window. Je viens de manger is a few minutes ago, not yesterday. For yesterday you need the passé composé, coming in Chapter 8.

Au Québec

Three things you'll hear on a Friday afternoon in Montréal. Understand them. On the exam, use the standard forms.

  • Je vas for je vais. Casual speech has Je vas faire les courses. It's old French and perfectly normal there. Write je vais.
  • La fin de semaine is the weekend. Qu'est-ce que tu fais en fin de semaine ? is the Québec version of Simon's question. ce week-end works everywhere, including in Québec.
  • magasiner means to go shopping. Je vais magasiner au centre-ville. On the exam, say faire les courses (groceries) or faire les magasins (shops).

Bonus: a car is often un char in Québec French, so en char is "by car". Click the sentences to hear a Canadian voice, if your browser has one.

Exam warm-up: a message to a friend

The first writing task on the TCF Canada is a short message to a friend or relative. Check the current word limits and timing on the France Éducation international site before you book. For this warm-up, aim for 60 to 80 words.

Your task: it's Friday. Write a message to your friend Simon and propose a plan for the weekend. Say where you want to go, when, and how you'll get there. Use tu, because he's a friend. Write it on paper or in a notes app, and read it aloud when you're done.

Checklist. A good message has all of these:

  • At least three near-future sentences with aller + infinitive.
  • At least one place with au, à la, à l' or aux.
  • How you travel: en métro, en bus, à pied...
  • At least one time word: samedi, ce soir, demain...
  • One use of faire (an activity or the weather).
  • One question to Simon (Tu viens ?), and a greeting and sign-off.
A model answer

Salut Simon, qu'est-ce que tu fais samedi ? Moi, je vais aller au cinéma à huit heures. Après le film, nous allons manger au restaurant. Je vais arriver en métro, parce que je n'ai pas de voiture. Tu viens avec moi ? Dimanche, il va faire beau : nous allons faire une promenade au parc. Je viens de regarder la météo, alors c'est sûr ! À samedi, Ka-yan

That's about 65 words. Now check yours:

  • Did the second verb stay in the infinitive every time (je vais aller, not je vais vais or je vais allé)?
  • Did you write au cinéma, never à le cinéma?
  • Is faites spelled with -tes if you used it with vous?
  • Is the weather an il fait sentence, not je fais?

BULLET POINTS

  • aller: je vais, tu vas, il va, nous‿allons, vous‿allez, ils vont. Ça va ? is this verb. ils vont has no liaison, so it differs from ils‿ont by one consonant.
  • Contractions are compulsory: à + le = au, à + les = aux, de + le = du, de + les = des. la and l' never merge.
  • Places: à Montréal (city), au Canada (masculine country), en France (feminine country), aux États-Unis (plural). Transport: en métro, en bus, en voiture, à pied, à vélo.
  • faire: je fais, tu fais, il fait, nous faisons, vous faites, ils font. faisons sounds like "fuh-zon". Never faisez. Weather: Il fait froid / chaud / beau. J'ai froid is a feeling, Il fait froid is the weather.
  • venir: je viens, tu viens, il vient, nous venons, vous venez, ils viennent. il vient is nasal, ils viennent has a real n. Je viens de Hong Kong uses de / du / des for origin.
  • Futur proche: aller + infinitive. Je vais travailler demain. Negative: Je ne vais pas travailler. This is the future of everyday speech.
  • Passé récent: venir de + infinitive. Je viens de manger. It's 啱啱 / 剛剛. A place after de means "from", an infinitive means "just".
  • Time words: aujourd'hui, demain, ce soir, ce week-end, samedi, la semaine prochaine. samedi is this Saturday, le samedi is every Saturday.
