Chapter 1

Sounds first: the French mouth

French spelling is a liar. Luckily, it lies the same way every time.

Ka-yan, first week in MontréalOne "croy-sant" and a coffee, please. Oh, and how do I get to "Saint Dennis" Street?

BaristaUn croissant, un café... Saint-Denis ? C'est tout droit !

Ka-yan, thinkingShe said "san-duh-NEE". Where did the T go? And the S?

Ka-yan reads French like English, and English rules don't work here. Before you learn a single word of grammar, you need to know how French letters turn into sounds. If you get this right now, every later chapter gets easier, and you won't spend next year unlearning bad habits.

Most of TEF/TCF listening is decoding sounds into words you already know.

Good news: you have a head start. You speak a language where each syllable gets its own clean beat, and where vowels don't slide around. English speakers have to learn both of those from scratch.

Rule 1: the end of the word is (usually) silent

Look at this sentence. The handwritten notes show which letters you don't say.

Click the whole thing: Les petits chats sont gris. ("The little cats are grey.") Five words, seven letters you never pronounce.

Silent letters are commented-out code: still in the source, never executed.

Most final consonants are silent. The big exceptions are the four in CaReFuL:

Final letterUsually...Examples
Cpronouncedavec, parc, sac
Rpronouncedfinir, pour, hiver
Fpronouncedchef, neuf, actif
Lpronouncedsel, avril, journal
anything elsesilentsalut, trop, nez, nous, grand

Watch it!

-er at the end of a verb is not CaReFuL. The R is silent and the ending sounds like é: parler, manger, aimer. Same for premier and boulanger. Thousands of verbs end in -er, so this exception matters more than the rule.

Adding an -e wakes the consonant up. petit has a silent t, but petite ends with a clear t. grand / grande. vert / verte. That's how you hear the difference between masculine and feminine in Chapter 3.

Rule 2: French vowels don't slide

Say the English word no slowly. Your mouth starts at "o" and slides to "oo": no-oo. Say say: it slides from "e" to "ee". English vowels glide. French vowels hold one position from start to finish, like a held note.

中文腦：你嘅元音本身已經好「實」

粵語同普通話嘅單元音都唔會滑，例如「媽」(maa1 / mā)、「衣」(ji1 / yī)、「夫」(fu1 / fū)。法文元音就係咁：一個口形，由頭到尾唔變。

要小心嘅反而係英文習慣。如果你讀 café 讀成英文 "ca-fay"，尾音滑咗去 "y"，法國人一聽就知。é 似「你」nei5 嘅開頭，但係停喺度，唔好收去「i」：口形由頭到尾唔郁。

There are no Dumb Questions

Do I have to learn those symbols in square brackets?
No, but they're worth a glance. They're the IPA, the alphabet dictionaries use for sounds. Wiktionary and Le Robert both show it, so when you look up a new word you can see exactly how it's said, without guessing from the spelling.
How do I know if an "o" is closed [o] or open [ɔ]?
Roughly: at the very end of a word, or spelled au, eau or ô, it's closed (vélo, tôt). Followed by a pronounced consonant, it's usually open (porte, bol). Mixing them up rarely changes the meaning, so don't lose sleep over it.
Why is u such a big deal?
Because u and ou make different words. tu means "you", tout means "all". dessus is "on top", dessous is "underneath". English speakers fight this one for years. You already have it.

Rule 3: nasal vowels (the "n" you don't say)

French has three vowels that come out partly through your nose. They're spelled as a vowel plus n or m, but the n or m itself is never pronounced. Your tongue never touches the roof of your mouth.

SpellingSoundExamples
an, am, en, em[ɑ̃]enfant, chambre, temps, lent
on, om[ɔ̃]bon, nom, maison
in, im, ain, ein, un[ɛ̃]vin, simple, pain, plein, un

The catch: it's only nasal when the n/m ends the syllable. If a vowel or a second n/m comes right after it, you get a normal vowel and a real "n" sound.

bonnasal, no n → bonne"bon-n", real n    unnasal → une"ün", real n

Same trick as Rule 1: an -e at the end changes the sound before it.

中文腦：-n 同 -ng 唔好收口

粵語「班」baan1、「邦」bong1 嘅韻尾 -n 同 -ng 會完全收口：舌頭頂到上顎。法文鼻元音唔會。

試下咁做：讀「邦」bong1，但喺舌根頂上去之前停低，氣由鼻出。咁就好接近 bon。

講普通話嘅朋友：好多人讀「昂」áng、「當」dāng 嗰陣個元音已經帶鼻音，保留嗰個元音、唔好收 ng，就係 an。

Watch it!

-ent at the end of a verb is completely silent. Ils parlent ("they speak") sounds exactly like il parle ("he speaks"), with no nasal and no t. But lent (slow) and souvent (often) aren't verbs, so they keep the nasal [ɑ̃].

Rule 4: the French R lives in your throat

The English R is made with the tip of your tongue. The French R [ʁ] is made at the very back, near where you'd gargle. The tip of your tongue stays relaxed behind your bottom teeth.

中文腦：用普通話嘅「h」

普通話「喝」hē、「河」hé 嘅 h 係喺喉嚨後面擦出嚟嘅音。法文 R 就喺同一個位置，再後少少，再加聲帶震動。

粵語冇呢個音。試下輕輕漱口，或者慢慢讀「家」gaa1，舌根唔好完全頂實，等啲氣擦過去。

千祈唔好用英文 R，亦唔好讀成 L：riz（米）同 lit（床）係兩個字。

Practise, slowly, then faster: rue · rouge · Paris · très · merci · quatre · Il rit.

中文腦：兩個香港口音陷阱

1. n / l 懶音。好多香港人將「你」nei5 讀成 lei5。法文唔可以：nez（鼻）≠ les，nous（我哋）≠ loup（狼）。

2. 尾音要「放」。粵語「一」jat1、「六」luk6、「十」sap6 嘅 t、k、p 唔會放出嚟。法文要讀出嚟嘅尾輔音（例如 sept、avec）要清楚放出，但唔好加多個元音變成 "sept-uh"。

Rule 5: words glue together

French hates two vowel sounds bumping into each other. It fixes the problem in two ways.

Elision: drop the vowel, add an apostrophe

Short words ending in e or a lose that vowel before a word starting with a vowel or a silent h:

le ami → l'ami · je ai → j'ai · la heure → l'heure · ne aime pas → n'aime pas · que il → qu'il

Elision isn't optional. "le ami" is a spelling mistake.

Liaison: a silent letter comes back to life

A silent final consonant gets pronounced when the next word starts with a vowel sound. It moves over and joins the next word. An s or x comes back as a "z".

Les amis"lé-za-mi" ont un"on-tun" hôtel.h is silent, so liaison happens

Click it: Les amis ont un hôtel. ("The friends have a hotel.")

Always linkNever link
les, des, un, mon, ton, son + noun: des‿enfantsafter et ("and"): un café et un thé, no link after et
pronoun + verb: nous‿avons, ils‿ontbefore an h aspiré: les héros, les haricots
adjective + noun: petit‿ami, deux‿heuresafter a singular noun: un chat énorme

There are no Dumb Questions

Both homme and héros start with h. How do I know which h blocks liaison?
You can't tell from the spelling. Dictionaries mark the blocking kind (h aspiré) with a symbol, often * or '. There are only a few common ones: le héros, la honte, le haricot, le hamburger, and the very Canadian le hockey. Notice they keep le or la whole, never l'.
Will my accent lower my TEF or TCF score?
Not by itself. Speaking graders care whether they can understand you without effort. A Chinese or English accent is fine. What costs you is a sound mistake that changes the word, like rue for roue or a missing nasal, because then the grader has to guess.
Is un a different sound from in?
It used to be everywhere. Most people in France now say them the same, and that's what we teach. In Québec many speakers still keep them apart, so you'll hear both.

Rule 6: one rhythm, stress at the end

English stresses one syllable per word and squashes the rest: VAN-cou-ver, MON-tre-al. French gives every syllable about the same length and puts a little extra weight on the last syllable of each group of words.

Montréal is "mon-ré-AL". J'habite à Vancouver. leans on the very last syllable, "-VER", not on "VAN".

中文腦：節奏啱，但唔好加聲調

粵語同普通話都係一個音節一拍，所以法文平均嘅節奏對你嚟講好自然，呢方面你贏英文母語嘅人。

要小心嘅係聲調。法文冇聲調：每個音節音高差唔多平，只係句尾（或者一組字嘅尾）先升或者降。唔好將 merci 讀成兩個有聲調嘅字。

Au Québec

Three things you'll hear on your first day in Montréal:

  • t and d before i or u turn into "ts" and "dz". tu sounds like "tsu", dire like "dzire", petit like "p'tsi". In Cantonese terms, Québec tu is close to 處 (cyu3).
  • Long vowels stretch and slide. père can sound like "pa-èr", fête like "fa-ète".
  • Casual words change. moi and toi can be "moé" and "toé" between friends.

Try: Tu dis que c'est petit ? (This uses a Canadian voice if your browser has one.) Understand all of this, but on the exam use the standard sounds from this chapter.

BULLET POINTS

  • Final consonants are usually silent, except C, R, F, L (CaReFuL). The r of an -er verb is silent (it sounds like é), and the verb ending -ent is silent altogether.
  • An -e at the end wakes up the consonant before it: petit / petite.
  • French vowels hold one position. Don't let café glide into "ca-fay".
  • u is 書 / 魚, ou is 夫, eu is 靴. Mixing u and ou changes the word.
  • Nasal vowels: the n/m is not pronounced, unless a vowel or another n/m follows.
  • R is made in the throat, never with the tongue tip. Keep n and l apart.
  • Elision (l'ami) is compulsory. Liaison links a silent consonant to the next vowel, but never after et.
  • Even rhythm, light stress at the end of each word group, no tones.
