Chapter 2

Être, avoir and you

Two small verbs hold up almost all of French. Meet them before they meet you at a government counter.

Ka-yan, Service Canada, MontréalI practised this on the bus. I have an appointment. How do I say "I have"? ...Oh, it's "I am", right? Same thing.

AgentBonjour, madame. Vous avez un rendez-vous ?

Ka-yanOui ! Je suis un rendez-vous !

AgentPardon ? Ah ! Vous avez un rendez-vous. Très bien.

Ka-yan, thinkingI just told a government employee that I'm an appointment. A small calendar entry, standing at her counter.

The agent asked "Do you have an appointment?" Ka-yan answered "I am an appointment." One wrong verb, one very polite smile. The agent understood her anyway. Real people are kinder than the exam is.

This chapter fixes the mix-up for good. You'll learn the two verbs you'll use every day, être ("to be") and avoir ("to have"), plus the little words that go in front of them. By the end you can introduce yourself, give your age, and say you're hungry without announcing that you are hunger.

No grammar from here on works without these two verbs. Treat this chapter as the foundation, not the warm-up.

Two quick points. First, on is how French people say "we" when they talk: On est à Montréal. ("We're in Montréal.") It takes the same verb form as il, so on est and il est match. nous is correct, but it sounds stiff in a chat.

Second, ils beats elles. A group of nine women is elles. Add one man, or a mixed group where you don't know, and it becomes ils. The rule is old, and it hasn't changed.

tu or vous?

English has one "you", so your instinct is to use one French word for everybody. Don't. Here's the rule that keeps you out of trouble.

  • vous for strangers, shop staff, officials, your boss, anyone older whom you don't know, and always when you're talking to more than one person.
  • tu for family, friends, children, classmates, and anyone who says On se tutoie ? ("Shall we use tu?").
  • On the exam: use vous unless the task says you're talking to a friend. A grader won't punish a polite vous.

Watch it!

Watch it!

ils sont and ils ont differ by one sound. sont starts with an s: "il-son". ont links the pronoun with a z: "il-zon". That z is the entire difference between "they are" and "they have". In fast speech it's easy to miss, so train your ear on it now.

Have hunger, have cold

Age isn't the only place where French says "have" and English says "be". A whole group of everyday feelings works this way. You don't are hungry. You have hunger.

FrenchLiterallyEnglish
J'ai faim.I have hunger.I'm hungry.
J'ai soif.I have thirst.I'm thirsty.
J'ai chaud.I have warm.I'm hot.
J'ai froid.I have cold.I'm cold.
J'ai peur.I have fear.I'm scared.
J'ai besoin de café.I have need of coffee.I need coffee.
Tu as raison.You have reason.You're right.
Tu as tort.You have wrong.You're wrong.

A few sounds to watch. The s in besoin sits between two vowels, so it's a z: "buh-zwɛ̃". The f in soif is said (CaReFuL). The t of tort is silent but its r is said.

Watch it!

J'ai chaud means "I'm hot". Je suis chaud says something else entirely, so don't reach for être here. With these expressions it's always avoir, never être.

You already know this

English does use "have" for some of this: "I have a cold", "I have a fever", "I have the shivers". French just does it for more feelings. And two of the words are cognates. raison is "reason" (to have reason on your side), and faim is related to "famine".

There are no Dumb Questions

Why do I need the pronoun? Doesn't the verb already show who it is?
Sometimes. Nous sommes can only be "we". But tu es and il est sound the same, and in Chapter 1 you saw il parle and ils parlent sound the same too. In speech the pronoun does a lot of the work, so French always says it.
Is on "we" or "one"?
Both. On est à Montréal means "we're in Montréal" in speech and "one is in Montréal" in a textbook. It acts as a singular, so it takes est, not sommes. In the speaking exam on is fine. In formal writing, prefer nous.
Can il and elle also mean "it"?
Yes. Every French noun is masculine or feminine, so il and elle also mean "it". A table is elle. Chapter 3 is all about this.

Au Québec

Three things you'll hear in Montréal. Understand them, but on the exam stay with the standard forms.

  • Tu is more common. Québec uses tu far more than France does. Classmates, colleagues and even some shop staff will say it from the start. A good rule: let them go first, then follow. With officials and older people, stay with vous.
  • Allô. Allô ! works as "hi" in person, not only on the phone. Allô ! Ça va bien ?
  • Pas pire. The usual answer to Ça va ? is Pas pire, literally "not worse", meaning "pretty good". The standard version, pas mal, works everywhere.

Try: Allô ! Ça va bien ? Pas pire, et toi ?

BULLET POINTS

  • Greet with bonjour (day) or bonsoir (evening). Salut is for friends. Close with au revoir and merci.
  • Nine pronouns: je, tu, il, elle, on, nous, vous, ils, elles. on is "we" in speech and takes the il form. A mixed group is ils.
  • vous for strangers, staff, bosses and groups, and on the exam. tu for friends, family and kids. Québec uses tu more.
  • être: je suis, tu es, il est, nous sommes, vous‿êtes, ils sont. tu es and il est sound the same.
  • avoir: j'ai, tu as, il a, nous‿avons, vous‿avez, ils‿ont. ils sont (s) and ils‿ont (z) differ only by that z.
  • No article before a job or nationality after être: Je suis ingénieur.
  • Age uses avoir and ans: J'ai trente ans. Numbers 0 to 20 are in the table.
  • Feelings use avoir: faim, soif, chaud, froid, peur, besoin de, raison.
  • Fixed phrases for now: Je m'appelle..., Je viens de..., J'habite à....
